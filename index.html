About

Why ConnectedSE

Many features of connected software are cross-layer. A single user-visible function can cross a mobile app, a vendor or manufacturer cloud, device firmware, and a physical device or vehicle, each developed and updated independently.

Many failures, vulnerabilities, and privacy leaks arise at the boundaries between these layers rather than inside any one of them. The stakes are concrete. In one study, all 77 wireless dongles that connect a phone app to a vehicle's internal network were found vulnerable, with attacks ranging from privacy leakage to property theft and safety threats. Large language models (LLMs) matter at a boundary for two reasons. They are the first tools that read the artifacts on both sides together, such as app code, firmware, protocol traces, and vendor documentation. They are also most likely to be wrong exactly there, because the far side is often unobservable, so their conclusions must be validated against behavior that no single artifact contains.

These systems share one structure. The mobile app is the layer through which users reach almost all of them, and the one that any research group can obtain and instrument. A cloud mediates, firmware and a physical device or vehicle close the chain, and an LLM-based assistant may sit on top as one more layer. ConnectedSE therefore treats mobile, IoT, and automotive software as layers of one problem rather than as separate domains. Its unit of study is the boundary between two layers.

Our guiding question. What evidence is needed to make a reliable claim about a feature whose behavior no single layer contains?
Call for Papers

Three themes, one boundary

The workshop is organized around the three things engineers do at a boundary between layers. Each one is tied to a kind of claim and to the evidence that claim requires.

1

Recover what crosses a boundary

Techniques that connect GUI actions, app code, APIs, cloud services, firmware, protocols, and execution traces, recover dependencies and data flows, or interpret undocumented interfaces.

Evidence problem: linkage
2

Exercise a boundary

Test generation, state exploration, oracle construction, fault localization, and repair validation across app, cloud, device, and vehicle updates, with simulators and digital twins as oracles for the state on the far side.

Evidence problem: oracles
3

Assure a boundary

Checking security, privacy, and compliance obligations when the far side is opaque, and keeping LLM-based layers such as smart-home agents and in-car assistants within their authorization, with decisions traceable to observable effects.

Evidence problem: defensibility
Every submission carries a boundary statement. In a few lines, name the neighbouring layers your result depends on, say how each was treated (real, mocked, emulated, simulated, or ignored), and say what would change if that assumption failed. This is what makes a mobile-only, firmware-only, or vehicle-only study comparable with a cross-layer one.
A shared deliverable. Benchmarks, datasets, testbeds, digital twins, replications, negative results, and comparisons with non-LLM baselines are welcome under every theme. The boundary statements of the accepted contributions are the starting material for a boundary map of the field, which we will draw together at the workshop. It shows which boundaries are studied, which assumptions recur, and which candidate tasks a reproducible cross-layer benchmark should contain.

Scope

We have two reference settings. In companion-app ecosystems, including smart homes and wearables, an app, a vendor cloud, and device firmware share a feature. In connected vehicles, the vehicle, manufacturer cloud services and over-the-air update infrastructure, mobile apps, in-car LLM assistants, and digital twins form the same structure. We also welcome work on mobile software and on automotive software engineering more broadly, including standalone mobile apps, in-vehicle software, and the simulation-based testing of driving functions. Medical devices and industrial IoT are further instances.

One boundary is enough. Work reasoning across app and cloud, cloud and device, app and device, or app and external services does not need to cover the whole chain. Single-artifact studies are in scope when they make their relationship to ecosystem behavior explicit or provide a substantive advance for LLM-assisted mobile software engineering.

Topics of interest include, but are not limited to

  • Recovery of dependencies and data flows across apps, clouds, firmware, and vehicles
  • Interpretation of undocumented APIs and protocols
  • LLM-guided GUI, API, and protocol testing and fuzzing
  • Oracles for remote and physical state, including simulators and digital twins
  • The gap between simulated and real behavior in device and vehicle testing
  • Companion apps, digital keys, dongles, and telematics as paths from the phone to the vehicle
  • Fault localization and repair across app, cloud, and firmware updates
  • Privacy and security compliance checking (e.g., GDPR, Cyber Resilience Act, UN Regulations 155 and 156)
  • Analysis of opaque layers such as vendor clouds, closed firmware, and over-the-air updates
  • Authorization and traceability of LLM agents and in-car assistants
  • Testing and benchmarking of in-car LLM assistants
  • Benchmarks, datasets, testbeds, replications, negative results, and industrial experience reports

Submission

Contribution types

All accepted contributions are published in the ACM Digital Library and presented at the workshop. Page limits include references. In line with the FSE 2027 call, the workshop is not an alternative venue for full research papers, and we encourage short contributions.

Extended abstracts

Up to 5 pages, free of article processing charges. Research, experience, replication studies, negative results, tools, datasets, and benchmarks at an early stage. A mature evaluation is not required.

Vision and position extended abstracts

Up to 3 pages, free of article processing charges. A direction, a problem, or an argument that the community should discuss.

Long papers

Up to 8 pages. For work that needs the space, such as replication studies and benchmarks. Long papers follow the ACM Open policy, under which authors outside participating institutions pay an article processing charge.

Guidelines

  • Submissions must follow the ACM format adopted by FSE 2027. Formatting details and the submission link will be published here.
  • Every submission includes a short boundary statement, as described in the Call for Papers.
  • All submissions undergo double-anonymous review by at least three program committee members. A long paper may be accepted as an extended abstract.
  • Reviewing criteria are relevance, technical credibility, clarity, evidence appropriate to the contribution type, and ability to spark discussion. Relevance is judged by how useful the result is to researchers working on another layer. At comparable quality, cross-layer work has priority over single-layer studies.
  • Sessions are organized by problem rather than by domain, and each is chaired by a researcher from a different layer. The day ends with a boundary-mapping session, and a best-paper award is given at the closing.
  • For a contribution to appear in the proceedings, at least one of its authors must register for the workshop.
  • FSE 2027 is an in-person event, and each accepted contribution must be presented on site.
  • Following the SIGSOFT policy, the organizers and their directly supervised students, postdocs, and employees do not submit to ConnectedSE.
  • As a published ACM author, you and your co-authors are subject to all ACM Publications Policies, including ACM's new Publications Policy on Research Involving Human Participants and Subjects.
